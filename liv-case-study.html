Preserving Stories That Shape Us

Picture a first-generation child sitting at the dinner table, wanting to learn about their family's journey to America, but not knowing how to start the conversation. The questions feel too big, too personal, too complicated.

"Stories don't last. It makes me sad when I talk to my uncles because their stories about getting to Pakistan are crazy—sickness, hunger, the forest, and so much fear—but I don't know them well enough to explain them. I worry that they'll get lost forever."

This is where countless family stories remain untold, and where cultural heritage slowly fades with each passing generation.

Research & Discovery

I conducted comprehensive research using mixed methods to understand the barriers preventing intergenerational storytelling in immigrant families and identify opportunities for design intervention.

In-Depth Interviews (n=6)

Qualitative interviews with immigrant families to explore the emotional and cultural barriers to sharing family history and migration stories.

Auto-ethnography

Drawing from my own experience as a first-generation child to understand the nuanced challenges and cultural sensitivities involved.

Analysis of existing tools like StoryCorps and HistoryPin to identify gaps specifically for immigrant families' unique needs.

Interview Insights

Oral Storytelling Dependency

Most respondents learn about family history through verbal conversations with limited written documentation, making stories vulnerable to memory loss.

Cultural Trauma Barriers

Some parents resist discussing the past due to trauma from migration, war, or loss, creating silence that children interpret as unwillingness to share.

Language as Cultural Bridge

Language is deeply tied to cultural identity, but translation difficulties can lead to loss of meaning, especially with cultural nuances and emotional depth.

Need for Structured Conversations

Families want guidance on how to have these conversations but lack practical tools to organize and preserve the stories that emerge.

Documentation as Solution

Many respondents believe documentation would help preserve family history but don't know where to start or what tools to use.

Desire for Interactive Approach

Rather than static documentation, participants wanted community-based, interactive approaches that make storytelling feel natural and engaging.

Competitive Analysis

StoryCorps

Not tailored to first-generation or immigrant experiences. Stories are archived publicly, which can create hesitancy for families dealing with sensitive topics.

HistoryPin

More geographic-based than personal. Lacks structured ways to initiate family conversations and focuses more on community history.

Family Tree Apps

Focus on genealogical data rather than story preservation. Don't address the conversational barriers that prevent story sharing.

Research Synthesis

Through my research, three interconnected barriers emerged: families lack conversation frameworks, cultural trauma creates emotional hesitancy, and there's no easy way to document stories once they're shared. What became clear was that families desperately want to preserve their stories but don't know how to start.

The Desire vs. Action Gap

While 38% of respondents felt uncomfortable asking parents about their past, the majority expressed interest in a tool to facilitate these conversations. The barrier wasn't lack of interest; it was intimidation. Families needed structured prompts that made starting sensitive conversations feel less daunting.

Physical Experience, Permanent Record

"A book would be great, but I wouldn't want it to just be documented and forgotten. I'd love for stories to be revisited, maybe at family gatherings, making it more of a community project." — Interview Participant

This sentiment echoed across interviews. Participants wanted physical, communal experiences paired with permanent preservation. The solution needed to honor both.

Translation Isn't Transparent

"Somali isn't an easy language to translate because it tends to be more descriptive. Certain idioms, phrases, and entire stories don't carry the same meaning when translated." — Interview Participant

Participants consistently described how cultural meaning gets lost in translation. This insight shaped my decision to capture stories in their original languages rather than forcing English documentation.

These insights pointed to my design opportunity: create a tool that structures conversations while honoring cultural sensitivity and ensures stories aren't lost to time.

How Might We...

Help first-generation children and their parents facilitate meaningful conversations to obtain and preserve family history?

Solution: The Living Archive

An interactive card game paired with a mobile app that helps first-generation children spark conversations with their families. Each card contains carefully crafted questions designed to encourage storytelling, paired with NFC technology that seamlessly records these precious moments.

How It Works

The cards facilitate conversation while the app preserves it. Physical cards provide structured prompts and create shared tactile experiences, while the mobile app captures, transcribes, and organizes these stories into a permanent family archive.

Why Physical Cards?

Early in my design process, I considered a purely digital solution. But interview insights pointed in a different direction. Multiple participants described feeling uncomfortable asking direct questions about their parents' past, with one noting it felt "too much like an interrogation."

Physical cards solve this by reframing the interaction. Instead of a child asking pointed questions, families sit together and draw cards, transforming what could feel confrontational into a shared, game-like activity. The tactile nature of shuffling, selecting, and passing cards creates ritual and signals importance. Additionally, older parents might feel overwhelmed by app interfaces, but everyone understands how to pick up a card.

Physical vs Digital Balance

We wanted to create a form that allows children and their parents to communicate without distractions, but recognized that the absence of a digital solution leaves room for family history to be lost. The hybrid approach bridges this gap: physical cards facilitate focused, meaningful conversation while seamless digital recording ensures these precious stories are preserved for future generations.

Why NFC Technology?

I chose NFC over alternatives like QR codes or manual recording for seamless interaction. QR codes require holding the phone up to scan, which interrupts conversation flow and feels transactional. NFC creates an almost effortless moment: tap the card, recording starts automatically, conversation continues. The technology disappears into the background, supporting rather than interrupting human connection.

Question Design Strategy

Questions were carefully phrased to encourage storytelling and bring up topics that might not emerge in everyday conversation. Instead of direct interrogation, each prompt invites narrative and reflection.

Instead of asking "When did you first feel comfortable in America?", this prompt invites storytelling through narrative. It focuses on a specific moment rather than requesting analysis, making the response feel more personal and accessible.

User Workflow

Users scan the QR code on the physical card deck to link questions with their app account. After logging in, they begin tapping cards on their phone. For the first question of each session, users are prompted to identify speakers. The app then transcribes conversations and highlights each person speaking, allowing seamless continuation by tapping the next card. All stories remain private to the family with no external sharing or public archiving.

Impact & Key Learnings

Bridging Generations

The hybrid physical-digital approach successfully accommodates different generational comfort levels with technology while creating meaningful shared experiences.

Cultural Sensitivity in Design

Learned the importance of trauma-informed design when working with immigrant families. Questions needed to encourage sharing while respecting boundaries around difficult topics.

Research Method Insights

Auto-ethnography proved invaluable for understanding emotional nuances that surveys couldn't capture, while mixed methods provided both depth and scale.

Next Steps & Future Development
